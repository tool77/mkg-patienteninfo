Patient Information
Tooth Extraction · Single Tooth Removal
Dear patient, this document explains the removal of a single tooth. It helps you prepare for the personal informed-consent discussion and summarizes the procedure, alternatives, anesthesia options, risks, and behavior after the procedure. Please read the page once completely before your appointment.
1 · Why should the tooth be removed?
Common reasons
  • Deep tooth decay: The tooth structure is so severely destroyed that stable reconstruction is no longer sensible.
  • Root crack: A crack extends into the root and makes the tooth non-restorable in the long term.
  • Advanced periodontitis: Bone support is severely reduced, and the tooth is loose or repeatedly inflamed.
  • Uncontrolled inflammation: Root inflammation or an abscess cannot be adequately treated with tooth-preserving measures.
  • Prosthetic or orthodontic reason: The tooth stands in the way of a sensible overall treatment plan.
What happens if nothing is done?

Leaving a non-restorable tooth in place is generally possible, but depending on the findings it can have significant disadvantages.

  • Recurrent pain or inflammation
  • Spread of infection into bone, soft tissue, or the maxillary sinus
  • Further bone loss in the future implant or denture area
  • Damage to neighboring teeth or more difficult later restoration
  • Acute worsening at an unfavorable time
2 · What alternatives are there?
Tooth preservation

If the tooth can still be preserved sensibly, root canal treatment, root canal revision, periodontal therapy, a build-up, or a crown may be alternatives depending on the findings.

Leaving the tooth in place

No treatment is possible if you decide against the procedure. However, the cause and risks remain. The individual balance of risks and benefits is explained during the consultation.

Second opinion

If it is unclear whether the tooth can be preserved, or if several equivalent treatment paths are possible, discussion with your general dentist or a second opinion may be useful.

Important: Removing the tooth is not a decision against later restoration. It is often the necessary first step to stop inflammation, pain, or an unstable situation.
3 · How is the tooth removed?
Simple extraction

After complete local anesthesia, the tooth is carefully loosened with special instruments and then removed. You feel pressure and movement, but no pain. The wound is then cleaned, bleeding control is checked, and sutures are placed if needed.

Surgical extraction

If the tooth is deeply destroyed, has several roots, is strongly curved, or is firmly anchored in the bone, it is removed gently by a surgical approach. The gum may need to be opened. If necessary, we remove some bone and divide the tooth into several pieces.

Change of plan during the procedure: Sometimes it only becomes clear after the start that simple removal is not safe or gentle enough. In that case, the procedure is changed to a surgical extraction. The goal remains the same: as little pressure as possible and controlled removal.

5 · General risks and complications
Postoperative bleeding rare, but important Slightly blood-tinged saliva on the day of surgery is common. True postoperative bleeding means that fresh blood continues to run and cannot be stopped by constant pressure.
Dry socket typically from day 2-5 The protective blood clot dissolves too early or does not form stably. This can cause deep-seated pain radiating to the ear and a bad taste. Smoking especially increases the risk.
Infection Despite careful removal, the wound can become inflamed. Warning signs include increasing swelling, warmth, pus, fever, or clearly increasing pain after initial improvement.
Swelling, bruising, limited mouth opening The more surgical the removal, the more likely swelling, a bruise, or limited mouth opening can occur. This is usually temporary.
Root fragment or retained root tip Strongly curved, very thin, or previously treated root parts can separate. In rare cases, small root remnants may be left in place if removing them would be riskier than monitoring them.
Neighboring tooth, filling, crown Neighboring teeth, crowns, bridges, or fillings can rarely be stressed or damaged, especially if the tooth to be removed is very tightly positioned or severely destroyed.
Bone edge or small bone particles After healing, small sharp bone edges or bone particles may become noticeable. Often this smooths out on its own; sometimes it is checked or smoothed.
Very rare risks Allergic reactions, circulation reactions, swallowing small fragments, jaw fracture in severely weakened bone, or pronounced inflammation are very rare, but must be mentioned.
6 · Special risks depending on tooth region
Upper posterior teeth

The roots can be close to the maxillary sinus. If an oroantral connection (MAV) is opened, it is usually closed immediately. After that, special rules apply: do not blow your nose, sneeze with your mouth open, and avoid pressure equalization.

Lower posterior teeth

The lower jaw nerve may run near the roots. Temporary numbness of the lip, chin, or tongue is possible. Permanent sensory disturbance is rare.

Front tooth area

If a visible gap will occur, a temporary prosthesis should be organized with your general dentist before the extraction appointment. We cannot produce such a temporary prosthesis spontaneously on the day of surgery.

7 · Pre-existing conditions and medication
Please always tell us
  • Blood thinners: Marcumar, Xarelto, Eliquis, Lixiana, Pradaxa, long-term ASA medication, clopidogrel
  • Bisphosphonates, denosumab, romosozumab, or antiangiogenic medication
  • Diabetes, immunosuppression, long-term cortisone therapy
  • Radiation therapy in the head and neck region
  • Heart valve replacement, endocarditis risk, relevant allergies
Why this matters

These factors change bleeding, infection, or wound-healing risks. Antiresorptive medication can rarely promote jawbone necrosis. Blood thinners must not be stopped on your own; any adjustment is made only after medical coordination.

8 · Behavior after the procedure
First hours
  • Bite on the gauze with constant pressure for 30-60 minutes
  • Keep your upper body elevated and rest physically
  • Do not keep checking, do not spit, do not rinse vigorously
  • Eat only when the local anesthesia has completely worn off
Cooling and rest
  • Cool damp and mildly; no ice pack directly on the skin
  • Cooling intervals: about 10 minutes cooling, 10 minutes pause
  • Rest on the day of surgery, no sport, no heavy lifting
  • Reduce exertion for 7-10 days depending on healing
Protect the blood clot
  • No straw, no sports bottle with suction
  • Do not suck on the wound or manipulate it with your tongue
  • No alcohol, nicotine, coffee, or energy drinks for at least 3-5 days
  • Prefer soft, cool, or lukewarm food

Tip: If you have already received a prescription, taking the first pain tablet about 1 hour before the procedure can be useful. After that, take pain medication according to the plan and not on an empty stomach.
Oral hygiene

Continue brushing the other teeth carefully, but avoid the wound at first. On the day of surgery, do not rinse or gargle vigorously. From the following day, a mouth rinse or a lukewarm salt-water mouth bath can be used gently if it was recommended to you.

No oral irrigator: Do not use an oral irrigator and do not use a rinsing syringe unless we have explicitly shown it to you or given it to you.
10 · Gap and later restoration
Implant

An implant replaces the tooth root and later carries the crown. Sufficient bone must be present. In some cases, alveolar ridge preservation or later bone augmentation is useful.

Bridge

A bridge can be a fixed alternative. Often, neighboring teeth have to be prepared for this. Planning is carried out with your general dentist.

Denture or no restoration

A removable restoration or initially no restoration may be sensible depending on the situation. In the long term, neighboring teeth can tilt or migrate if a gap remains open.

Visible front tooth gap: If a visible gap will occur and you want a temporary prosthesis, it must be prepared by your general dentist before the extraction appointment. Please coordinate this early with that practice.

12 · Frequently asked questions
Does tooth removal hurt?

Not during the procedure. You feel pressure and movement. If you feel pain, give a hand signal immediately; then additional local anesthetic is given.

Are sutures always necessary?

No. After simple extractions, the wound often heals without sutures. With surgical removal, MAV risk, bleeding risk, or a larger wound, sutures are more likely.

How long will I be limited?

After a simple extraction often 1-2 days; after surgical removal more likely several days. Physical work, sport, and sauna should be paused depending on healing.

What is dry socket?

The blood clot protects the wound. If it is missing or breaks down, bone is exposed. This is painful but treatable. Increasing, radiating pain from day 2-5 is typical.

When may I eat again?

Only when the local anesthesia has completely worn off. Start with soft, cool, or lukewarm food and do not chew directly on the wound.

Who plans the tooth replacement?

The definitive restoration is usually planned with your general dentist. We discuss whether anything should be prepared surgically for the later restoration.

Contact us immediately or seek assessment in case of ...
  • Heavy postoperative bleeding that does not stop despite 30 minutes of constant pressure
  • Increasing pain from day 2-5 despite pain medication
  • Fever above 38.5 °C or lasting longer than 2 days
  • Pus, foul smell, or bad taste
  • Increasing swelling after initial improvement
  • Persistent numbness of lip, chin, or tongue
  • Fluid from the nose after upper posterior tooth extraction
  • Severe swallowing difficulty or shortness of breath
For problems after the procedure, first use the postoperative emergency number we gave you. If we cannot be reached: dental emergency service 01805 60 70 11. Only in a life-threatening emergency, shortness of breath, severe swallowing difficulty, or rapid swelling of the floor of the mouth, neck, or tongue: call 112 immediately.
