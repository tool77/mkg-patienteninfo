Wisdom Tooth Removal - Final Handout Version
Dear patient, this document explains all important points concerning your wisdom tooth removal - from preparation and the procedure itself through to complete healing. Reading time is about 8-10 minutes. Please read the whole page once before your appointment. It does not replace your personal informed-consent discussion, but it fully summarizes all information discussed.
1 · Why do wisdom teeth have to be removed?
Common reasons for removal
  • Lack of space & pressure: angled wisdom teeth press against neighboring teeth and can permanently damage them
  • Difficult hygiene: the very back areas are hard to clean, leading to tooth decay and gum inflammation
  • Mucosal injuries: upper wisdom teeth growing sideways can chronically rub the cheek
  • Cyst formation: displaced teeth can form cysts that weaken the jawbone
  • Acute inflammation: recurrent pericoronitis / gum pockets around the wisdom tooth
What happens if nothing is done?

Leaving the tooth in place is generally possible, but we do not recommend it when there is a clear indication. Possible consequences include:

  • Progressive inflammation spreading into the surrounding tissue
  • Bone loss at the neighboring tooth (tooth 7 is often affected)
  • Growth of a cyst with increasing weakening of the bone
  • With increasing age, the procedure and healing become more complex
  • Over the age of 35, the risk of complications rises significantly
Favorable timing: Between about 14 and 25 years of age, removal is often a responsible option when a risk constellation is visible: healing is usually less complicated, and later damage to neighboring teeth or recurrent inflammation can be avoided.
Why we are more likely to recommend removal in teenagers and young adults: If the X-ray shows that wisdom teeth are present, do not have enough space, and are unlikely to erupt normally into the dental arch, we often recommend removal at around 14-25 years of age. In this situation, the decisive point is not the mere presence of the tooth, but the visible risk constellation: lack of space, partial retention, unfavorable angulation, difficult hygiene, recurrent inflammation, or impending damage to the neighboring tooth. With increasing age, the balance shifts: without symptoms and without visible damage, observation is more likely; with pain, inflammation, tooth decay, cyst formation, or damage to the neighboring tooth, removal is therapeutic.
2 · Preparation before the procedure
Pain medication in advance

Ideally, take the prescribed pain medication 1 hour before the procedure. It is already active in the tissue when the local anesthetic wears off after the procedure, which considerably reduces postoperative pain.

No aspirin / ASA!

Avoid aspirin (ASA) and other blood-thinning pain medication for about 1 week before the procedure. They massively increase the risk of postoperative bleeding. For pain, take ibuprofen or paracetamol.

Antibiotic prophylaxis

In certain cases (pre-existing conditions, increased infection risk), preventive antibiotic intake before the procedure is useful. Whether this is necessary for you was discussed with you individually.

3 · How the procedure is performed
Upper jaw

The gum is opened carefully. The bone in the upper jaw is softer and more elastic; depending on the tooth position, bone is removed minimally and gently. In most cases, the tooth is then loosened and removed directly - a comparatively uncomplicated procedure.

The wound is closed with a few stitches. The sutures are removed after 1-2 weeks at the follow-up appointment.

Lower jaw

In the lower jaw, the gum is also opened carefully. The lower jawbone is denser. To remove the tooth gently and avoid strong pressure, we remove a minimal amount of bone and, if necessary, divide the tooth into segments that are then lifted out individually.

The wound is then closed with a few stitches. The sutures are removed after 1-2 weeks at the follow-up appointment.

Operating time: When all four wisdom teeth are removed, the actual operating time is usually about 20-30 minutes in total. The entire appointment takes about 60 minutes because the preliminary discussion, local anesthesia or anesthesia preparation, and final discussion are included.
One session or one side at a time: If all four wisdom teeth have to be removed, both approaches are possible. However, the presumed advantage of removing one side at a time is often smaller than expected: the individual treatment is shorter, and in theory chewing on the opposite side may be easier. Because the lower jaw moves as one unit, mouth opening and chewing are often limited even after a one-sided operation. Therefore, if there is no medical reason against it, we usually recommend removing all four wisdom teeth in one session. This is especially sensible with twilight sedation or general anesthesia, because anesthesia preparation, monitoring, recovery phase, and swelling have to be managed only once.
Bone healing: The bone defect created by tooth removal fills completely with bone again within about 6 months. No bony scars and no increased risk of fracture remain.

Your options at a glance

5 · Possible risks and complications
Nerve injury (lower jaw) - temporary more common, permanent < 0.1% The lower jaw nerve (inferior alveolar nerve) runs directly beneath the roots. Irritation can cause temporary numbness of the lower lip, chin, or tongue. Permanent damage is very rare. There is no visible paralysis.
Opening of the maxillary sinus (upper jaw) - 5-10% Upper jaw roots often anatomically extend close to the maxillary sinus. If this is opened during removal, this is anatomy-related and not a treatment error. We close the opening immediately with a suture. After surgery: do not blow your nose for 14 days; only gently sniff. Sneeze only with your mouth wide open.
Wound-healing disorder - "dry socket" - 2-4% (smokers up to 20%) The protective blood clot dissolves too early and the bone is exposed. Severe pain from day 3-5. Easily treatable with special dressings. Most important prevention: do not smoke.
Infection - 2-5% Can occur despite careful technique. Signs: swelling with warmth and redness, pus, rising fever. Usually treatable well with antibiotics.
Postoperative bleeding - rare Slight oozing and blood-tinged saliva in the first hours is normal. If bleeding remains heavy: bite firmly on gauze (30 minutes), then present again if needed.
Neighboring teeth & root remnants - < 0.5% Very rarely, neighboring teeth (tooth 7) are touched. Small root remnants can be left in the bone and monitored if removal would carry too high a risk.
Jaw stiffness - temporarily common, pronounced cases rare Limited mouth opening due to swelling and muscle irritation is normal in the first few days and resolves on its own.
Very rare risks - < 0.01% Jaw fracture (only with severely displaced teeth, weeks after surgery), allergic reaction to anesthetics, accidental swallowing of tooth fragments.
Special notes for pre-existing conditions

The following section is not relevant for the majority of young, healthy patients. Older patients or patients with pre-existing conditions should inform us before the procedure:

Blood thinners (anticoagulation) Marcumar, Xarelto, Eliquis, Lixiana, Pradaxa, long-term ASA medication, clopidogrel. INR adjustment before the procedure may be necessary; we coordinate this with your physician. Do not stop medication on your own.
Bisphosphonates / denosumab For example due to osteoporosis or tumor disease - even if stopped years ago. Increased risk of jawbone necrosis (MRONJ). Please tell us the medication name, duration of use, and last intake.
Diabetes Increased susceptibility to wound-healing problems and dry socket. Please provide your current HbA1c value if known.
Immunosuppression / cortisone / radiation therapy Increased infection risk. Radiation therapy in the head and neck region increases the long-term risk of necrosis. Please inform us fully.
6 · Behavior after the procedure
Immediate measures
  • Hold the bite gauze firmly for 30-60 minutes
  • Keep your upper body elevated and rest
  • Do not spit and do not rinse vigorously
  • No straw - suction opens the wound
  • Eat only when the numbness has completely worn off
Really keep the bite gauze constant: Please bite firmly for 30-60 minutes and do not keep checking. Each removal can dislodge the fresh blood clot, so bleeding starts again.
Smoking, alcohol, coffee, energy drinks: avoid for at least 3 days, preferably 1 week. Smoking leads to an alveolitis risk of up to 20%.
Cooling - the right way
  • Damp, hand-warm/cool cloth (about 17 °C) - 10-15 minutes, pause, repeat
  • No ice, no cold packs! Excessive cooling promotes postoperative bleeding and increases swelling
  • Swelling is normal, peak on day 2-3
  • Bruising on the cheek is also normal
  • Swelling subsides on its own after day 3
Why no ice? Cooling that is too cold first causes tissue overcooling. As soon as it is removed, the body strongly increases blood flow to the area. This can promote bruising, postoperative bleeding, and additional swelling.
Nutrition
  • Day 1-2: liquid - soup, smoothie, yogurt, quark
  • Day 3-5: soft - puree, pasta, fish
  • From day 6: normal according to how you feel
  • Avoid hot, spicy, crumbly foods
  • Target: 1,500-2,000 kcal/day
  • Dairy products (yogurt, quark, cheese) are expressly allowed
  • No straw · Do not chew on the surgical side
Please eat and drink enough: Too little energy and fluid increases the risk of circulation problems, nausea, vomiting, poorer pain-medication tolerance, stronger pain, and delayed wound healing.

Frequently asked questions
Will there permanently be a gap in the jaw?
No. The gum is sutured and grows together quickly. The bone defect created fills completely with bone within about 6 months. No bony scars and no increased risk of fracture remain.
What does health insurance pay for?
Statutory health insurance covers the procedure, X-ray, and local anesthesia. Oral tablet sedation, twilight sedation, and general anesthesia are optional private co-payments (approx. EUR 80-450).
How long will I be on sick leave?
You receive a sick note on the day of surgery. Office work: approx. 3-5 days. Physical work: approx. 7-10 days. This varies individually.
What if the numbness remains permanently?
Temporary numbness after lower jaw procedures is common and usually resolves completely within weeks to months. Permanent damage is very rare (less than 0.1%). If sensation is not improving after 4-6 weeks, please contact us - we will refer you for neurological assessment if needed.
When may I do sport again?
Light activity (walking) is possible from day 3-5. Sport with an increased pulse (jogging, fitness, swimming) only after at least 7 days - after extensive procedures or bone work after 10-14 days. Contact sports and diving: after complete wound healing (approx. 3-4 weeks).
Should wisdom teeth be removed preventively?
Not simply because a wisdom tooth is present. However, if X-ray and findings show that there is too little space and a risk constellation exists - for example partial retention, unfavorable angulation, difficult hygiene, recurrent inflammation, caries/periodontitis risk, cyst formation, or impending damage to the neighboring tooth - removal at around 14-25 years of age is often sensible. With increasing age, observation is more likely if there are no symptoms or visible damage; with symptoms, inflammation, or damage, removal is therapeutic.
Call immediately if ...
  • Heavy postoperative bleeding - gauze does not help after 30 minutes
  • Increasing pain from day 3-5 despite pain medication
  • Swelling increases after day 3-4 instead of subsiding
  • Fever above 38.5 °C or lasting longer than 2 days
  • Pus formation or very foul smell / taste
  • Persistent numbness of lip, chin, or tongue
  • Jaw stiffness that does not improve
For problems after the procedure, first use the postoperative emergency number we gave you. If we cannot be reached: dental emergency service 01805 60 70 11. Only in a life-threatening emergency, shortness of breath, severe swallowing difficulty, or rapid swelling of the floor of the mouth, neck, or tongue: call 112 immediately.
What you should take away after reading
Important: This summary does not replace the full information above. Please read the whole page once so you can ask specific questions during the informed-consent discussion.
  • Removal is recommended when wisdom teeth have too little space or there is a risk of inflammation, damage to neighboring teeth, cysts, or hygiene problems.
  • All four wisdom teeth can be removed one side at a time or in one session; if there is no reason against it, one session is often sensible, especially with twilight sedation or general anesthesia.
  • The actual operating time for four wisdom teeth is usually about 20-30 minutes; the entire appointment about 60 minutes.
  • Local anesthesia is the standard. Sedation or general anesthesia are comfort options with accompanying person, collection from the practice, and special fasting rules.
  • After the procedure, constant biting on the gauze, correct cooling, sufficient eating and drinking, and not smoking are especially important.
  • Immediate assessment is necessary in case of heavy postoperative bleeding, increasing swelling, fever, pus, increasing pain, or breathing/swallowing problems. After the procedure, first use our postoperative emergency number; if we cannot be reached: dental emergency service 01805 60 70 11.
